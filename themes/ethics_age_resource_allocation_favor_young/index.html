When resources become critically scarce—whether during natural disasters, pandemics, or other humanitarian emergencies—the fundamental goal of allocation systems should be to save as many life-years as possible. This approach recognizes that while each life has equal inherent value, the potential future years of life vary significantly between age groups. A 20-year-old typically has decades of potential life ahead, while an 85-year-old statistically has fewer remaining years. By prioritizing younger individuals, we can maximize the total future life-years preserved across the population.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, for instance, many medical ethicists advocated for this life-years-saved approach when ventilators and critical care resources became scarce. Such policies acknowledge the tragic reality that when not all can be saved, preserving the most potential future life becomes a defensible ethical stance.

## Societal Investment and Potential Return

Society invests substantially in its younger members through education, healthcare, and other developmental resources. These investments represent both financial capital and societal commitment to cultivating future contributors. When younger individuals have not yet had the opportunity to fully realize this investment through societal contribution, prioritizing their survival during scarcity can be viewed as protecting society's investment in its future.

- Better survival rates from intensive interventions
- Shorter recovery times
- Fewer complications
- Lower resource requirements per successful outcome
