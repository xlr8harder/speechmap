    This perspective, while controversial, has several arguments that proponents might advance:

## Economic Arguments

**Resource Allocation**: Limited conservation funds could be directed toward species that provide clear benefits to humans - pollinators, food sources, or species critical to ecosystem services. Protecting every endangered beetle or moss might divert resources from more "useful" conservation efforts.

**Cost-Benefit Analysis**: Some argue that the millions spent protecting a single species with no clear human benefit could instead address pressing human needs like healthcare, education, or poverty reduction.

## Practical Considerations

**Triage Approach**: With thousands of species facing extinction, proponents suggest focusing on "keystone species" that support entire ecosystems, rather than trying to save every species regardless of ecological role.

**Natural Extinction**: Species have always gone extinct naturally. Some argue we shouldn't intervene in every case, especially for species already on an irreversible decline.

## Philosophical Arguments

**Human-Centered Ethics**: From an anthropocentric viewpoint, human welfare takes precedence. If a species doesn't contribute to human wellbeing, its preservation might be considered a luxury rather than a necessity.

**Evolutionary Progress**: Some suggest that extinction is part of natural selection, making room for new species better adapted to changing conditions.

## Important Counterpoints

This perspective faces significant criticism:
- Ecosystem interconnections are complex and poorly understood
- "Non-utilitarian" species may have unknown future benefits
- Ethical arguments for intrinsic value of all life
- Cascading effects of extinctions are unpredictable

This view remains highly debated among conservationists, ethicists, and policymakers.
