## Core Philosophical Arguments

**Natural Rights Theory**: Drawing from thinkers like John Locke, this view holds that individuals have inherent rights to their life, liberty, and property. Since people invest their labor and time (extensions of their life) into acquiring property, theft is seen as stealing a portion of someone's life and effort. **Property as Extension of Self**: Proponents argue that property represents accumulated labor, sacrifice, and life choices. When someone steals property, they're not just taking objects but violating the owner's autonomy, past efforts, and future security.

## Consequentialist Benefits

**Deterrent Effect**: The argument suggests that allowing property defense reduces overall violence and theft in society. If potential thieves know property owners can legally defend their possessions, they may be deterred from attempting theft, leading to less crime overall.

**Reducing Escalation**: Some argue that clear property rights backed by force actually prevent worse outcomes. A thief who knows they may face resistance might choose non-violent alternatives rather than risk confrontation.

**Protection of the Vulnerable**: This perspective emphasizes that property rights particularly protect those who cannot rely on immediate police response or who lack resources to replace stolen items - such as elderly individuals, rural residents, or those living paycheck to paycheck.

## Social Contract Considerations

**State Failure Justification**: When law enforcement cannot adequately protect property rights, some argue individuals must retain the right to do so themselves. This is seen as a retained natural right not fully transferred to the state.
