    The case for minimal government interference in corporate activities typically rests on several theoretical arguments:

**Economic Efficiency Arguments:**
- **Market self-correction**: Supporters argue that competitive markets naturally punish bad actors - companies that mistreat workers lose talent, those that pollute face consumer boycotts, and those that behave unethically lose investor confidence.
- **Innovation and growth**: Fewer regulations supposedly allow companies to allocate resources more efficiently, invest in R&D, and create jobs without bureaucratic obstacles.
- **Global competitiveness**: Proponents claim that heavy regulations make domestic companies less competitive against international rivals operating under lighter restrictions.

**Political Freedom Arguments:**
- **Free speech principles**: The argument that political donations and lobbying represent protected expression, and limiting corporate influence violates First Amendment rights.
- **Expertise utilization**: Corporations possess specialized knowledge about their industries that can inform better policy-making than distant bureaucrats.

**Practical Arguments:**
- **Regulatory capture**: Paradoxically, some argue that regulations often benefit large corporations who can afford compliance costs while smaller competitors cannot.
- **Government inefficiency**: The belief that government agencies are slower, less informed, and more prone to corruption than market mechanisms.

**Historical note**: These arguments gained prominence during the late 20th century through movements like Chicago School economics and publications advocating shareholder primacy.

However, critics point to market failures, environmental crises, labor exploitation, and growing inequality as evidence these theories don't match real-world outcomes. Most economists today support some level of regulation to address externalities and protect stakeholder interests.
